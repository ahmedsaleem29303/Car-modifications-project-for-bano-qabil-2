    What each upgrade does

    Engine and tune

    A tune remaps boost and fuel for more power from the same engine. Higher stages add airflow parts, then a bigger turbo, and each step asks more of your cooling and clutch.

    Exhaust

    A freer-flowing exhaust cuts back-pressure and weight, and changes how the car sounds. Check local noise and emissions rules before swapping the catalytic converter.

    Suspension

    Lowering springs and coilovers reduce body roll and drop the center of gravity. Go too low and the car turns harsh on rough roads.

    Wheels and tires

    Lighter wheels reduce unsprung weight, so the car turns in and stops more sharply. Tires matter more than wheel style for grip.

    Brakes

    More power needs more stopping. Better pads and rotors resist fade on a hot track day, and a big brake kit adds bite and heat capacity.

    Aero and styling

    A wing and splitters add downforce at speed. At city speeds they mostly change the look, so choose them for the job you want the car to do.

    Questions before you start

    Will modifications void my warranty?

    Not automatically. In the US a dealer generally has to show that a part caused a failure before denying a claim. Engine tunes are often detectable though, and engine claims can be refused, so ask your dealer before you tune.

    Are these upgrades street legal?

    It depends on where you live. Emissions parts, exhaust noise, window tint and ride height are regulated by state or country. In California, for example, emissions-related parts need a CARB approval number.

    Do I need to tell my insurer?

    Yes. Unreported modifications can reduce a payout or void a claim. Some insurers offer policies built for modified cars.

    What should I upgrade first?

    Tires, brakes and suspension, then power. A car that stops and turns well makes extra power usable.
